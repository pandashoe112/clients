# Strip the BS

## The rule

A heading describes what is in the section. It is not a headline, an opinion, or
a joke.

Write the heading a person needs in order to find that content: **subject +
place**, or **subject + action**, in the words a customer would actually use,
with the search term in it where one exists.

## Why this matters more than it sounds

Most people arrive at a client's page cold, from Google, part-way down, on a
phone, deciding in about two seconds whether this business does the thing they
need. A clever heading costs them that decision. It also carries no search term,
so the page ranks for nothing.

Headline-style headings feel like better writing because they read well *in
sequence*, top to bottom, the way the writer reads them. Nobody reads a page
that way. They scan the bold text looking for their own problem.

## The four tests

Apply these to every heading before it ships:

1. **Cold-arrival test** — could a stranger who has read nothing else on the
   page tell what is underneath this heading? If not, rewrite.
2. **Portability test** — would this heading still make sense above a different
   section, or on a competitor's site? If yes, it says nothing. Rewrite.
3. **Search test** — is the thing a customer would type actually in the words?
   "Electrical work in Kensington homes" contains the search. "The hardest
   suburb we work in" contains none of it.
4. **Table-of-contents test** — pull every heading on the page into a list and
   read just that list. It should work as a summary of the page. If the list
   reads like a series of slogans, the headings are wrong even where each one
   seemed fine on its own.

## Banned shapes

These are the forms the fluff keeps arriving in:

- Statement plus a twist after a comma or dash — "The hardest suburb we work in,
  and we like it"
- Opinion or boast in place of description — "Electrical work done properly"
- Rhetorical questions — "Wondering what's behind your walls?"
- "We" framing, where the business is the subject instead of the customer's job
- Wordplay, alliteration, puns, "Where X meets Y"
- Vague abstractions — "Our approach", "The difference", "What sets us apart"

## Real before and after

From five Revelectrical suburb pages that had to be rewritten:

| Was | Now |
| --- | --- |
| The hardest suburb we work in, and we like it | Electrical work in Coburg homes |
| 1930s wiring behind a 2020s kitchen | Electrical work in Northcote homes |
| Two Kensingtons, two different jobs | Electrical work in Kensington homes |

The replacements are boring on purpose. Boring is legible, and it is what the
six pages that were never broken already used — consistency across a set of
pages is itself part of the point.

## Body copy, same rule

The heading is where it shows worst, but the same standard applies underneath:

- Say the thing, then stop. No throat-clearing sentence before the real one.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions: "switchboard upgrade", not "electrical
  solutions".
- Cut any sentence that would be equally true of any competitor.
- Claims carry a number or a name, or they get cut. "48 five-star reviews" and
  "licensed electrician, REC 12345" survive. "Trusted by locals" does not.
- Plain English over industry vocabulary, unless the customer is searching for
  the industry word — then use it, because it is what they typed.

## Applying it to existing pages

When asked to review or fix a page, don't hand back prose about what's wrong.
List every heading in document order, and for each one give the replacement or
mark it as fine. Then make the edits. A table of was/now is the useful artifact
here, because the client can approve it at a glance without reading the page.

Keep the heading level structure intact while doing it — this is a copy change,
not a restructure, unless the structure is what's broken and you say so
explicitly.

## When not to apply this

Not everything is a service page. A brand campaign line, a tagline, a joke on a
404 page, or an internal document can carry personality. The test is whether
someone is trying to *find something* — if they are, describe. If the user asks
for something deliberately punchy, give them that and say you've set the rule
aside.
